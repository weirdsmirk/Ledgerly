# Anti-patterns

Use this reference as a diagnostic, not a prohibition list. Any pattern below can be appropriate when it follows from the product and is executed with care.

## The generic launch-page stack

Watch for the automatic sequence of announcement pill, centered headline, gradient-highlighted phrase, vague subcopy, two buttons, floating product mockup, logo strip, three feature cards, metrics, testimonials, FAQ, and oversized call to action.

The issue is not any single section. The issue is that the sequence can be produced before the agent knows what the product is. Start from the actual decision a visitor must make and the evidence needed to make it.

## Style as accumulated effects

Common symptoms include:

- purple-to-blue gradients used as a default signal of technology;
- translucent glass panels over decorative blobs;
- glow on every emphasized element;
- soft shadows, borders, and gradients all applied to the same surface;
- rounded rectangles nested several levels deep;
- a different visual trick in every section.

Choose an underlying material and depth model. Use effects only to express hierarchy, interaction, atmosphere, or a specific visual premise.

## Card soup

Cards are useful for discrete, comparable, movable, or actionable units. They are weak when every heading, paragraph, metric, control, and section receives its own floating container.

Before adding a card, ask whether grouping, alignment, spacing, a rule, or a change in background would communicate the relationship more directly.

If the live site already uses cards for features, FAQs, plans, or comparable units, those cards are brand. Do not delete them on a restyle. Removing every card is as automatic as wrapping everything in a card.

## The interchangeable hero

Symptoms include a centered message with no relationship to the page below, an abstract dashboard mockup, vague claims, and visual emphasis on a single fashionable word.

Let the hero demonstrate the product's actual value: an outcome, artifact, workflow, point of view, or useful interaction. A centered hero remains valid when ceremony, focus, or symmetry supports the message.

## Decorative data

Avoid made-up charts, metrics, activity feeds, avatars, logos, notifications, and testimonials whose only purpose is to make a layout look credible. They create false product meaning and push the design toward generic SaaS imagery.

Use real data, clearly marked representative data, or a composition that does not depend on fabricated proof.

## Default icon treatment

An icon inside a tinted rounded square above every heading is not automatically a component system. It often adds visual noise without adding information.

Use icons where recognition, scanning, action, or category distinction improves. Vary presentation when importance and behavior differ. Keep decorative icons secondary and accessible.

## Pill inflation

Pills imply compact status, selection, filtering, or a short self-contained label. Applying them to navigation, metadata, buttons, badges, and decorative captions can make every element feel equally interactive and equally important.

Use shape to communicate role. Do not let one fashionable radius erase component distinctions.

## Bento by reflex

Unequal grid cells can express hierarchy and relationships. A bento grid becomes generic when arbitrary spans are filled with disconnected micro-demos merely to occupy a fashionable composition.

Map size and position to information priority. If all items are equivalent, a simpler repeated structure may be more honest.

## Excessive centering

Centering can create focus, ceremony, and balance. Used everywhere, it weakens scanning, makes long copy awkward, and causes sections to lose a directional rhythm.

Choose alignment based on reading behavior and the relationship between text, media, and controls.

If the live site already centers section heads, FAQ blocks, or closing CTAs, that alignment is brand. Do not left-align the whole site to "avoid AI centering." Choose alignment per region. Do not pick one alignment for every section as a restyle move.

## Chrome vs decoration

Treat blobs, glows, and decorative dot grids as unused decoration unless the premise gives them a job. Treat Mac or browser window dots, URL bars, and product-in-browser frames as chrome with a job when the card is meant to look like a window or app. Do not remove traffic-light dots from existing browser mocks during a restyle unless the user asked to drop that metaphor.

## Timid sameness

A page can avoid flashy clichés and still feel generated through evenly sized sections, identical padding, uniform cards, neutral sans-serif type, and no strong hierarchy.

Introduce deliberate contrast in scale, density, alignment, pacing, or format. Do not add random novelty; make the contrast serve the content.

## Animation as polish

Scroll reveals, floating objects, animated gradients, and parallax do not compensate for weak hierarchy. Repeated movement can slow comprehension and ignore reduced-motion needs.

Use motion to explain change, preserve continuity, confirm action, or direct attention. Make the static frame successful first.

## Diagnostic questions

- Could this page belong to an unrelated product after changing the logo and accent color?
- Which visible decision came directly from the audience, content, or product behavior?
- Are components separated because their meaning differs, or because empty space needed filling?
- Is hierarchy expressed structurally, or mostly through glow, gradient, and shadow?
- Does the page still work after removing decorative effects?
- Are repeated patterns genuinely comparable and repeated, or merely made to match?
- What would an informed human designer find too automatic here?
