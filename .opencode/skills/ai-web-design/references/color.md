# Color

Color should clarify roles, establish atmosphere, and support recognition. A palette is a system of relationships, not a set of fashionable swatches.

## Begin with role and environment

Identify the product's setting, existing brand colors, content type, expected usage duration, and accessibility needs. A public arts archive, emergency console, wellness journal, and developer tool need different balances of energy and restraint.

Define roles before values:

- page and raised surfaces;
- primary and secondary text;
- subtle borders and dividers;
- primary action and focus;
- selection and hover;
- success, warning, error, and information;
- data-series colors when needed.

Keep the system as small as the interface allows.

## Construct tonal relationships

Choose neutrals with the intended temperature and contrast. Avoid adding a faint accent tint to every neutral surface unless that atmosphere is part of the premise. Ensure adjacent surfaces are distinguishable for the right reason—tone, border, elevation, or spacing—without stacking all four.

Use saturated color where attention or identity warrants it. If everything is accented, color no longer communicates priority.

## Make gradients earn their place

Gradients can represent light, depth, change, energy, or brand character. They become generic when used as the default technology aesthetic, especially purple-blue washes on headings, buttons, and glowing backgrounds.

If using a gradient, define what it contributes that a solid color cannot. Check banding, text contrast across the full field, and its behavior in dark mode.

## Design semantic colors as a set

Status colors need adequate contrast, distinct labels or icons, and consistent meanings. Do not rely on red versus green alone. Test subtle status surfaces and strong status controls separately.

## Treat dark mode as its own light environment

Do not merely invert values. Rebalance surface separation, saturation, image treatment, shadows, borders, and text contrast. Very bright accents can vibrate against dark surfaces; deep shadows may disappear entirely.

## Check contrast in context

Meet relevant accessibility contrast requirements, including interactive states and text over images or gradients. Test actual rendered font sizes and weights. Disabled states may be subdued but must remain understandable; placeholder text cannot carry essential meaning.

Also inspect color with simulated color-vision differences and in grayscale when color communicates structure.

## Quick review

- Does every color have a stable role?
- Is the accent reserved for meaningful emphasis?
- Are interactive and semantic states distinguishable without color alone?
- Do surfaces remain clear after removing shadows?
- Does dark mode feel composed rather than inverted?
- Is the palette specific to the product, not merely specific to current trends?
